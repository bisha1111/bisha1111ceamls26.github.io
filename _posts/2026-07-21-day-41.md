what_i_learned: |
  Today, our group focused on turning nine weeks of technical work into a clear story that an audience could easily follow. Instead of treating the deck as a collection of separate pages, we approached it as a guided explanation of how DermaBridge began, how it was built, and why the project matters.

  We examined the entire sequence from the opening problem to the closing message. We wanted viewers to first understand the barriers surrounding early skin-cancer screening before introducing our proposed solution. From there, we arranged the material to show how the medical-image data was gathered and prepared, how different algorithms were tested, what the evaluations revealed, and how those discoveries influenced the design of the DermaBridge prototype.

  A major part of the day involved deciding how to represent complicated information without overwhelming anyone. Dense explanations were shortened, technical details were translated into simpler language, and several pages were redesigned to place greater attention on the most important takeaways. We used graphs, model outputs, interface images, and other supporting material only where they added meaning to the explanation.

  We also reviewed the sections featuring the mobile experience. The interface images needed to demonstrate how a person could move through the system, provide relevant information, capture an image, receive an understandable assessment, and access guidance about seeking professional care. This helped connect the machine-learning portion of the project to a practical tool that people could actually interact with.

blockers: |
  One difficulty was finding the right balance between scientific depth and audience understanding. Our work involved many stages, and each stage contained details that felt important. However, including every procedure, measurement, and technical choice would make the talk difficult to absorb.

  We had to make careful decisions about which details belonged on the screen, which points could be explained verbally, and which information was not essential for the symposium. Several pages were crowded at first, so we reduced the amount of writing and gave the strongest findings more space.

  Another issue was connecting the analytical portion of the project with the application portion. We did not want the prototype to appear separate from the research. We rearranged the sequence to show that the app was shaped by the same goals that guided our technical work, including accessibility, explainability, fairness, and responsible referral.

  Some graphs and interface images also required adjustments because their labels were difficult to read from a distance. We enlarged important elements, removed unnecessary details, and repositioned items so that viewers could understand them without needing to study the screen closely.

reflection: |
  Today taught me that presenting scientific work requires a different type of problem-solving. Building a model or developing an application is only part of the responsibility. Researchers must also explain why they made certain choices, what their evidence means, and how the work connects to a real need.

  Looking through the full deck allowed me to recognize how many pieces had come together to form DermaBridge. What began as an idea for improving access to skin-cancer screening had developed into a project involving medical data, artificial intelligence, fairness analysis, explainable risk information, interface development, and healthcare referral support.

  I also saw how easily valuable work can lose its impact when it is presented without a clear structure. An audience should not have to search for the main point. Each page should guide them toward the next idea while keeping the larger purpose of the project visible.

  The collaboration within our group made the revision process stronger. Each team member noticed different issues and contributed suggestions based on their area of involvement. By listening to one another, we were able to create a more unified explanation of the project rather than presenting disconnected pieces of individual work.

  By the end of the day, the deck felt more focused, understandable, and visually balanced. It represented not only what we built, but also the reason behind it: using responsible technology to make early screening information more accessible while paying attention to fairness, trust, and the needs of underserved communities.
